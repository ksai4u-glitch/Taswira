# Hausa lore and the interlaced Arewa motif: checked creative evidence

Research date: 27 October 2026. Research only; no repository or GitHub writes. Sources below were opened and their relevant text read, except the explicitly marked follow-up leads. Page numbers refer to the printed page when available. Proposed graphics are editorial interpretations, never recovered historical insignia.

## Decision for Taswira

The user's interlaced forms are a valuable starting point for a Hausa visual identity. The evidence supports using a newly drawn knot as a **cultural/editorial identifier**, with that status visible in its metadata. It does **not** establish any of the four supplied variants as a documented flag, seal, or coat of arms of medieval Kano, Katsina, Daura, Zazzau, Gobir, Rano, or Biram. I have not been given the original files or their provenance in this subtask, so I cannot identify their individual creators, dates, or reproduction rights.

Three histories must remain distinct:

1. Interlace and knot designs in clothing and architecture, with older contexts and variable interpretations.
2. Dagin Arewa as a contemporary Hausa/Northern Nigerian cultural identifier.
3. Its modern political adoption in the 1950s under Ahmadu Bello, including use as a Northern Nigerian authority symbol. This adoption is not a medieval origin date for every interlaced motif.

For a medieval map, good label: **“Hausa cultural motif · editorial identifier”**. Avoid **“Flag of the Hausa Kingdoms”**, **“Ancient Hausa coat of arms”**, or a precise medieval date for the exact drawing. If different kingdoms receive distinct emblems, describe these as newly designed identifiers based on specified material or narrative evidence. Lore can enrich the map without inventing a historical heraldic system.

## Evidence register

### 1. Lam Museum: clothing motifs, tentative chronology, present identity

**Source:** [Contexts, Timothy S. Y. Lam Museum of Anthropology, Wake Forest University](https://lammuseum.wfu.edu/exhibits/virtual/cultures-contexts-collectors/contexts/). Curated by Dr. Andrew Gurstelle. Opened body lines 139–145.

**Object:** Hausa shoes, Nigeria, mid-20th century, accession 2018.07.E.08.a-b, Stanley P. Bohrer Collection.

**Passage:** “Artists likely began using these designs” during the 13th–16th-century trans-Saharan gold trade. The label connects knots with medieval Islamic protective practices and identifies dagin arewa as a present Hausa cultural symbol.

**What this permits:** Clothing-derived interlace and a cautiously worded account of possible medieval connections. **Limit:** “Likely” is the curator's inference; the exhibited object is mid-20th-century, not a dated medieval survival. The protective interpretation should be attributed to this context, not treated as the sole meaning for all knots. No evidence here for seven loops = seven Hausa states, eternity, ancestral bloodlines, or a universally fixed cosmology.

**Rights:** No open license for the photograph was established. Cite the object and curator; seek image permission or make an original, clearly credited interpretive drawing.

### 2. UBC Museum of Anthropology: an older robe, with uncertainty retained

**Source:** [baban riga, Cb81, more information](https://collection-online.moa.ubc.ca/search/item?place_made%5B0%5D=62129&row=18&tab=more), plus [object overview](https://collection-online.moa.ubc.ca/search/item?place_made%5B0%5D=62129&row=18). Read the iconography, techniques, physical description, dating and collection fields.

**Object:** Hausa man's robe, Kano, Nigeria; catalog date **“Before 1910 ?”**; acquired 3 August 1976; Mrs. Yvonne Adams Collection.

**Passage:** “Design motifs featured on this robe are dagi knots and the two-pointed knife (aska biyu) pattern.” The catalog links embroidery to Hausa architecture/decorative arts, status, and protection. It describes cotton-strip construction and extensive collaborative embroidery work.

**Use/limit:** This is object-based evidence for dagi and aska biyu as design vocabulary, with a potentially pre-1950 object. Preserve the catalog's question mark. The robe's general history back to the 11th century is not a date for this exact motif or object. Do not equate every dagi pattern with a single standardized political knot.

**Rights:** Overview credits image © UBC Museum of Anthropology, photograph Rebecca Pasch; site reserves rights. An open museum record is not a reusable-image license.

### 3. Moses Ochonu: the political adoption is specifically modern

**Source:** Moses Ochonu, [“Unity in Diversity: Palace Art in Nigeria”](https://quod.lib.umich.edu/j/jii/4750978.0015.206/--unity-in-diversity-palace-art-in-nigeria?rgn=main;view=fulltext), *The Journal of the International Institute* 15(2), Spring **2008**, p. 7. [One-page PDF, fully read](https://quod.lib.umich.edu/cgi/p/pod/dod-idx/unity-in-diversity-palace-art-in-nigeria.pdf?c=jii%3Bidno%3D4750978.0015.206%3Bformat%3Dpdf). Nigerian historian's account of a research visit to Dutse palace.

**Passage:** “The symbol was adopted in the 1950s” when elites prepared for independence. Ochonu identifies Ahmadu Bello's leadership, the “One North” political philosophy, and approval for a Northern Nigerian coat of arms/seal of authority. He describes its later invocation of Bello's power and Northern unity at Dutse.

**Use/limit:** Strong support for dating a **political appropriation**, not the invention of interlace. It does not by itself give an exact adoption day, official flag specification, or a chronology of every variant. Contemporary palace ornament may deliberately reconnect past and present; its presence on an old palace does not automatically make each painted motif medieval.

**Rights:** Copyrighted; Michigan's record permits linking and requires permission for redistribution. Reference the article and create original graphics; do not reuse its photographs by assumption.

### 4. ABU thesis: useful corroboration, but follow its citation back

**Source:** Theophilus Obioma Nwogu, [*An Examination of Traditional Mural Art of the Obingwa*](https://kubanni-backend.abu.edu.ng/server/api/core/bitstreams/b4440600-5b37-48cc-ac16-010b0f6ba0f6/content), M.A. dissertation, Ahmadu Bello University, December **2015**. Opened title/declaration and discussion around printed p. 39, figure xvi.

**Passage:** The discussion reproduces the Northern Knot and a description attributed to Ochonu, including 1950s political adoption and Bello's authority symbolism.

**Caveat:** This is a comparative literature-review passage in an Obingwa mural thesis, not original Hausa field research. It cites Ochonu as **2003**, whereas the located original article is clearly **2008**. Cite the original for this claim. Do not silently propagate the thesis's citation date or its image as licensed artwork.

**Rights:** No open reuse license established for thesis or reproduced figure.

### 5. Bayajidda, Daurama, Kusugu: local scholarship and variant traditions

**Source A:** Bashir Aliyu Sallau, Department of Hausa, Federal University Dutsin-Ma, [“Exploiting and Harnessing Tourism Potentials as a Source of Revenue Generation in Katsina State”](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/382220923_EXPLOITING_AND_HARNESSING_TOURISM_POTENTIALS_AS_A_SOURCE_OF_REVENUE_GENERATION_IN_KATSINA_STATE_BASHIR_ALIYU_SALLAU_PhD_DEPARTMENT_OF_HAUSA_FACULTY_OF_ARTS_FEDERAL_UNIVERSITY_DUTSIN-MA_KATSINA_STATE_-), *FUDMA @ 10*, chapter 13, pp. 223–237, **November 2020** (uploaded July 2024). Read title/publication notice, methodology and Kusugu section, pp. 224–225.

**Passage:** “According to historical tradition” introduces a seventh-century account of Daurama and Kusugu. Sallau links Bayajidda's snake-slaying with restored water access and notes palace regalia associated with the story.

**Use/limit:** Strong locally grounded narrative imagery: water, well, serpent, queen, regalia, community. Its date is explicitly traditional; do not promote it to independently verified archaeological chronology. Objects associated with a legendary hero do not prove the hero's biography. The author supplies no open license for photographs or text.

**Source B:** Abba Gana Wakil Mahamadou, attributed to University of Maiduguri's Centre for the Study and Promotion of Cultural Sustainability, [“Bayajidda Hausa Historical Legend: Myth or Reality”](https://www.csan-niger.com/bayajidda-hausa-historical-legend-myth-or-reality.php), 10 January **2020**. Read abstract and narrative sections, especially well/serpent sequence and discussion of palace transmission.

**Use/limit:** Names Alhasan Abdurrahman as a transmitter of a Daura palace account. Presents Bayajidda's arrival, a local host, serpent encounter, and the queen. It also advances speculative origin arguments; those should not enter a factual timeline. Use it to show that the tale has identifiable versions and tellers, not as proof of Baghdad migration or a single historical founder of all Hausa communities. Rights not established.

### 6. Amina: historical memory with competing chronologies

**Source A:** [*Littafin Tarihin Kano*, Hausa version of the Kano Chronicle](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/382793058_Littafin_Tarihin_Kano_Hausa_Kano_Chronicle), a reproduction of material under R. M. East's direction, **1933**, drawing on H. R. Palmer's **1908** publication; uploaded by Hausa scholar Abdalla Uba Adamu in August 2024. Read title and Dauda section, pp. 13–14.

**Passage read:** Under Dauda's heading **1421–1438**, the text places Amina's Zazzau ascendancy, tribute from Nupe, kola, and thirty-four years of power. This demonstrates the Chronicle's chronology, not proof that its dates are correct.

**Source B:** Professor Tijjani Muhammad Naniya, [“33 observations…”](https://nigeriantracker.com/2020/09/27/33-observations-on-the-claim-made-by-ndagi-abdullahi-regarding-the-non-existence-of-queen-amina-of-zazzau1533-1610-ad/), 27 September **2020**. Read points 1–10 and 29–33. Naniya insists on Zazzau as the polity, defends indigenous chronicles against categorical dismissal, and acknowledges historians' challenges to the Amina story.

**Source C, lower confidence overview:** Wardah Abbas, [Amaliah's Amina essay](https://www.amaliah.com/post/60307/muslim-women-in-islam-and-history-honouring-leadership-legacy-queen-amina-zaria), 20 April **2021**, “Conflicting Narratives” section, opened and read. It explicitly contrasts fifteenth-century Chronicle dating with the post-1576 reconstruction attributed to Abdullahi Smith, and provides a bibliography. Its broader article contains geographical/chronological problems, so use the dated disagreement as a research lead, not the article as an authority for armies, precise borders, armor, or the attribution of every surviving wall.

**Use/limit:** “Amina of Zazzau; dates disputed” is defensible in lore-focused content. A reign beginning in 1576 is common in another reconstruction, but conflicts with the Chronicle placement; do not animate a precise conquest sequence or border expansion from this material alone. Respect both the historical debate and her significance in Hausa memory. No verified lifetime portrait, personal emblem, exact armor, or sword was established here.

**Rights:** The ResearchGate reproduction says content may be copyrighted and its uploader recognizes NNPC's rights. Do not confuse a 2024 upload with the text's publication date or with an open license. Naniya's article is copyrighted journalism; short quotation/linking only.

### 7. Gizo and Zomo: distinct characters, not kingdom symbols

**Source A:** Yusuf Mohammed, [*Kwatancin Taurari Waniqiqi a Tatsuniyar Hausa da Fulatanci*](https://kubanni-backend.abu.edu.ng/server/api/core/bitstreams/98490b9f-0911-4f65-b2bd-ec2597c5cfa6/content), M.A. African Literature (Hausa), Ahmadu Bello University, **December 2014**. Read English/Hausa abstracts and Hausa character sections pp. 40–55.

**Passage:** The English abstract names “Gizo, Zomo and Dila” as Hausa tricksters. Separate sections treat Gizo and Zomo. Gizo's traits include cleverness but also selfishness, lies, greed, and folly; he is not an uncomplicated wise hero. The Zomo section includes the hare calling himself Muduka in a hyena-den food deception. The thesis also uses comparative international examples; check each story's provenance before treating it as specifically Hausa.

**Source B:** [*Hausa Folk-lore, Customs, Proverbs, etc.*, vol. II](https://archive.org/details/hausafolklorecus02rattuoft), **1913**, Clarendon Press; [full OCR text read](https://archive.org/stream/hausafolklorecus02rattuoft/hausafolklorecus02rattuoft_djvu.txt), tale 30, pp. 124–146. In the spider/hippo/elephant story, Gizo manipulates a long horse-rope and sets their followers pulling against each other; a later disguise lets him evade retaliation. Do not reassign this attested version to Zomo merely because other traditions have hare tug-of-war stories.

**Attribution:** Credit **Malam Shaihu's Hausa texts, collected/transliterated/translated by R. S. Rattray (1913)**, distinguishing teller/scribe from colonial translator. [A scanned Ajami proverb page and its attribution](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Introduction_to_Hausa_proverbs.jpg) were opened: it explicitly credits Shaihu's Arabic-script text and Rattray's Roman transliteration/English translation. The vol. I preface could not be fetched, so Shaihu's exact birth details or each tale's oral chain are not independently established in this research.

**Rights:** ABU thesis has no established open license. The 1913 English edition is public domain in the US; the Commons scan is marked public domain in life-plus-70 jurisdictions too. Check the particular scan/edition and deployment jurisdiction. Public domain status does not erase the need to credit Shaihu, Hausa tradition, and editorial mediation. Do not copy a contemporary children's-book retelling, illustration, or translation.

### 8. Bori: living religious practice, not a generic fantasy category

**Source:** Kari B. Henquinet, [“Bori Religion in West Africa”](https://doi.org/10.1093/acrefore/9780190277734.013.587), **20 March 2024**, *Oxford Research Encyclopedia of African History*. [Full abstract opened and read](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/379111343_Bori_Religion_in_West_Africa); full chapter unavailable.

**Passage:** The abstract describes a religious tradition evidenced by at least 1500 CE, with healing, possession, initiation, gendered authority, and varying relationships with Hausa aristocracies and Islam. It notes condemnation under the nineteenth-century Caliphate while practice continued.

**Use/limit:** Distinguish religious history from a tatsuniya animal tale. Do not give every Hausa actor a Bori spirit, invent a universal spirit hierarchy, or call all pre-Islamic Hausa people Bori practitioners. No particular spirit image, sacred insignia, performance choreography, or sound recording is cleared by this abstract. For actual depiction, seek named practitioners/local specialists and fuller scholarship. Copyrighted; facts and brief attributed discussion only.

## Five visual and storytelling directions grounded in this evidence

1. **The knot that moves between cloth and wall.** Draw an original interlace in a robe-panel frame, transitioning into architectural relief. Cite the UBC object for embroidery vocabulary and Ochonu for the later political chapter. In the map legend, call it an editorial Hausa identifier. Different kingdom badges can use a shared border but different researched local content. No invented meanings for individual loops.

2. **Daura: the well opens.** A circular well mouth, descending bucket line and serpent silhouette produce a legible tiny emblem and a short cinematic vignette. Show the queen and community as agents in the narrative, and name the selected storyteller/version. Caption it “Bayajidda and Daurama · Daura origin tradition”; do not attach a calendar year or treat its genealogy as a literal map of ethnic descent.

3. **Amina: power remembered in earth.** A restrained silhouette of an earthen enclosure and rider/commander can accompany a source card comparing the Chronicle placement with later dating. Label the image an interpretation, not Amina's portrait or seal. A strong scene is the tension between the scale of remembered power and the uncertainty of dates; exact conquest borders require separate political research.

4. **Gizo: the map can lie.** A small spider, one rope and two offscreen pulling forces make a distinctive story emblem. A reveal shows apparent bilateral struggle was engineered by a third actor. This is an interpretive visual analogy drawn from Shaihu/Rattray tale 30, not an explanation for an actual war. Keep political timeline events factual, and offer the folktale as an explicit side-story.

5. **Zomo: a name changes the outcome.** Hare ears at the mouth of a hyena den, with a deliberately confusing name token, support an interactive language-centered story about the Muduka deception recorded by Yusuf Mohammed. It complements Gizo while preserving the characters' separate identities. Use a Hausa language reviewer before publishing the pun or voice performance. Commission new narration and illustration; credit the exact tale source and adaptation.

## Follow-up priorities and exclusions

- A Nigerian specialist or Hausa-language editor should review spelling, voice, Ajami treatment and selected local versions. Qur'anic calligraphy, Ajami and decorative interlace are different kinds of content; do not generate pseudo-writing to fill a badge.
- Before any claim of an actual historical flag/seal, obtain a dated archival object or document for that polity and period. None was verified here.
- Useful scholarly follow-ups not read in full: S. B. Ahmad, *Narrator as Interpreter: Stability and Variation in Hausa Tales* (2002); Mary Wren Bivins on Daura/gender and Hausa historical narrative; Sa'ad Abubakar's Amina chapter in Bolanle Awe's *Nigerian Women in Historical Perspective*; fuller Bori studies by Nigerian scholar Michael Onwuejeogwu and I. H. Abdalla. Do not present their arguments as checked findings from this memo.
- National Library of Nigeria's digitized *New Era* interview with Emir Muhammadu Bashar of Daura surfaced in search but failed to open. It is a promising credited oral-history source, not evidence used above. Contemporary Daura official/tourism pages give conflicting very early dates; they should be read as heritage claims until independently supported.
- No source opened here supports giving the identical modern Arewa emblem to every precolonial polity across northern Nigeria, equating all Northern Nigerians with Hausa people, or merging Hausa, Kanuri, Fulani and other traditions into one timeless culture.
