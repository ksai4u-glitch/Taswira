# Taswira: animal guide and narrator options

Research checked 27 October 2026. This is an options memo, not a permanent brand selection. The animal, personality, costume, movement and screen behavior proposed below are **new creative proposals**; the cited folklore and zoological observations are **research findings**. No channel artwork, historical illustration or museum object has been copied or adapted.

## Recommendation for a first design round

Develop **a Hausa-rooted hare** and **a Hausa-rooted spider** as the two leading directions. The hare gives Taswira a readable, flexible guide with documented Hausa storytelling connections. The spider gives the strongest built-in visual metaphor for connecting people, places and evidence, with a documented Hausa character tradition. Keep **a hornbill** as a third visual alternative if the desired presence is calmer and more architectural. Its cultural evidence here is Senufo, not Hausa.

The useful influence from Kurzgesagt is the *job*: a recurring animal helps viewers enter difficult ideas, navigate a diagram, and recognize the channel. Taswira should create its own anatomy, proportions, eyes, face, costume system, gestures and timing. “A green version of their duck” would not establish a distinct character.

## What the sources establish

### Hausa hare and spider: direct and contemporary scholarly evidence

- **Mohammed Yusuf, Ahmadu Bello University, 2014**, *Kwatancin Taurari Waniqiqi a Tatsuniyar Hausa da Fulatanci*. The institutional repository abstract identifies Gizo, Zomo and Dila as Hausa trickster characters and compares them with Fula traditions. Checked passage: “the Hausa folktale have Gizo, Zomo and Dila.” This supports the Hausa connection; it does not establish that any one character represents all Hausa culture. The indexed repository abstract was available; the full thesis timed out and was not inspected. [Repository record and abstract](https://kubanni.abu.edu.ng/items/03146436-1139-4c29-89a6-2bc87699bac5).
- **A. J. N. Tremearne, 1913**, *Hausa Superstitions and Customs*, pp. 5, 31, 266–267. Checked p. 31 explicitly associates **zomo** with hare and **gizzo** with spider. Tale 37, *The Hare Outwits the Hyaena*, credits **U. G.**; p. 5 identifies him as **Umoru Gombe**. Hare uses disguise to recover meat taken by Hyena. The contents credit spider Tale 36 to **M.**, identified as **Mohamma**. [Full historical text](https://archive.org/stream/hausasuperstitio00tremuoft/hausasuperstitio00tremuoft_djvu.txt); [scan record](https://archive.org/details/hausasuperstitio00tremuoft).

**Mediation note:** Tremearne was a colonial collector. His introduction contains racist judgments; he also describes dependence on male soldiers and personal servants, so the collection is selective. Credit Umoru Gombe and Mohamma when discussing these tellings, alongside the collector/translator. These are recorded variants, not transparent access to an unchanging “African mythology.” Use the collection to locate and compare stories, with contemporary Hausa scholarship and speakers guiding any eventual adaptation.

### Hausa spider origin: a different recorded telling

**Maalam Shaihu**—also rendered Shaihua in the digital edition—provided manuscripts for R. Sutherland Rattray’s *Hausa Folk-Lore* (1913). Rattray’s author’s note says Shaihu wrote down material and translated some Arabic manuscripts into Hausa, followed by Rattray’s transliteration and English translation. Chapter 6 is an origin-of-the-spider story. This is not the same narrative as the recurring Gizo trickster tales. [Author’s note](https://sacred-texts.com/book/hausa-folk-lore/author-s-note); [chapter 6](https://sacred-texts.com/book/hausa-folk-lore/6-a-story-about-a-chief-and-how-his-sons-observed-his-funeral-and-the-origin-of-the-spider). The [Smithsonian catalog record](https://www.si.edu/es/object/hausa-folklore-twenty-one-folk-stories-hausa-people-africa-maalam-shaihu-and-translated-r-sutherland%3Asiris_sil_1094339) corroborates Shaihu’s role and the translation route.

**Mediation note:** Rattray was also a colonial official. Preserve Shaihu’s authorship rather than describing this as simply “Rattray’s stories.” Do not infer that every tale came from oral dictation: the source itself describes manuscript and translation work.

### Gizo is not a substitute name for Ananse

The **Getty vocabulary identifies Anansi/Ananse as an Akan character**; the Met documents Ananse in Akan court and proverb contexts. These sources do not make Ananse Hausa. Taswira can discuss relationships and comparisons between traditions while retaining their names, places and tellings. [Getty record](https://www.getty.edu/cona/CONAIconographyRecord.aspx?iconid=901002551); [Met, Akan spokesperson’s staff](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/314925).

### Tortoise: Yoruba evidence, not a Hausa claim

**Oyekan Owomoyela’s *Yoruba Trickster Tales* (1997)** gathers 23 tales about **Àjàpá**, the tortoise, and situates the character among Yoruba communities of western Nigeria and neighboring Bénin. The publisher describes varied traits, including resourcefulness, vanity and playfulness. Do not reduce this character to “the wise old African turtle.” [University of Nebraska Press](https://www.nebraskapress.unl.edu/nebraska-paperback/9780803286115/yoruba-trickster-tales/).

### Hornbill: specific Senufo evidence, with a museum caution

The University of Michigan Museum of Art identifies a Senufo hornbill motif and associates it with primordial beings and planting. However, the Met cautions that **sejen sculptures need not depict a specific bird species**; Senufo speakers have given multiple identifications. Some belong to Poro settings and refer to ancestry, authority and guardianship. This supports a carefully attributed discussion of particular Senufo forms, not a generic “African wisdom bird” claim. [UMMA heddle pulley](https://umma.umich.edu/objects/heddle-pulley-with-hornbill-motif-1971-2-40/); [Met, Bird (Sejen)](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/310818).

### African grey and fennec: zoology supports these options

- **African grey parrot:** Rachel Holman’s University of Michigan Animal Diversity Web account describes forest habitats, grey plumage/red tail, social behavior and learned vocal communication. Its 2008 taxonomy treats Timneh as a subspecies, so avoid repeating its taxonomic treatment or old conservation assessments without updating them. The limited facts used here are body appearance and learned vocalization, not mythology. [Animal Diversity Web](https://animaldiversity.org/accounts/Psittacus_erithacus/).
- **Fennec fox:** Smithsonian’s National Zoo places the species in North African desert environments and describes large ears used for heat loss and detecting prey. This gives a geographic and behavioral design basis. **No specific Hausa mythological link was verified here.** [Smithsonian species account](https://www.nationalzoo.si.edu/animals/fennec-fox).

## Six original character directions

All working descriptions below are proposals, not names from folklore. A folklore-rooted species need not make the mascot a literal depiction of the named traditional character. Give the original guide its own eventual name and state its inspiration honestly.

| Option | Original silhouette and palette proposal | Personality and narrator role | How it guides maps and diagrams | Strength and tradeoff |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Hausa-rooted hare** | Tall, lean silhouette; two narrow upright ears, one tilting at rest; angular haunches; warm sand muzzle; deep green body with a restrained gold ear stripe. A simple gold fastening and green field satchel, if accessories are needed. | Curious, quick-thinking, warm and prepared to correct itself. A researcher’s companion who asks a useful question, then helps uncover the answer. Wit comes from observation rather than deceit. | Walks along a trade route; ears frame a destination; unrolls a small map; taps a dated source card. The camera can follow its gaze without making it constantly bounce. | Best balance of Hausa connection, expressiveness and simple rigging. Risk: exaggerated teeth, giant eyes and relentless hopping make it feel aimed at young children; generic rabbit anatomy could weaken distinctiveness. |
| **2. Hausa-rooted spider** | Eight slender, visibly jointed legs around a compact kite-shaped green abdomen; two gold rings; two quiet forward-facing eyes. Distinguish it through the overall silhouette and leg spacing rather than a superhero emblem or oversized cute head. | Patient connector of evidence with dry humor; notices missing links and conflicting accounts. Be clear that its role as a reliable historical guide is Taswira’s invention, rather than claiming traditional Gizo was a neutral archivist. | Runs a single strand between a source and a map location; places dated nodes; untangles two conflated names; pulls a thread that expands into a timeline. Keep silk diagrams visibly illustrative, not evidence of historical connections by themselves. | Most distinctive connection-making vocabulary, with a direct Hausa storytelling tie. Risk: arachnophobia, small-screen leg clutter and a more demanding rig; trickster associations can complicate narrator trust. |
| **3. Yoruba-rooted tortoise** | A low, elongated shell with large plain facets; green shell, warm parchment face and narrow gold rim; deliberate head and foreleg gestures. Use an original contour, not a copied sculpture or existing cartoon turtle. | Patient, incisive and occasionally pleased with its own cleverness. Can test a claim and admit an error. Its patience is an invented personality, not a universal description of Àjàpá. | Shell becomes a temporary staging surface for map cards; foreleg points to a timeline; travels by scene transitions rather than spending screen time slowly walking across maps. | A grounded presence that can sustain adult documentary pacing. Risk: “old and slow means wise” is predictable; broader African connection comes through a specifically Yoruba source here, not the user’s Hausa focus. |
| **4. Hornbill** | Upright green torso, long clean tail and a bold curved gold bill; small restrained casque; two or three large feather planes. Choose a real species before the final model sheet. Avoid the beak-to-belly posture and carved geometry of Poro sculptures. | Composed observer with occasional surprise; a capable guide rather than an all-knowing sacred elder. | Flies above a map, lands beside a city marker, and uses bill direction and wing tips to guide attention. Folded wings create a calm resting pose. | Strong profile silhouette and natural overhead-map transitions. Risk: the bill can dominate close-ups; another bird mascot needs especially independent anatomy and motion. The checked cultural connection is Senufo, not Hausa. |
| **5. African grey parrot** | Long, angular wing silhouette, charcoal face and pale eye ring; grey-green shading; retain a small muted red tail for species recognition and reserve gold for the brand accessory. | Attentive listener who compares testimony and pronunciation; a deliberate speaker with a modest sense of humor. Its archival role is invented. | Carries a source card into frame, perches at an annotation, repeats a name accurately, then highlights who said it and when. | A natural speaking narrator and a forest-Africa alternative. Risk: “parroting” can imply uncritical repetition; pirate cues and constant squawks would undermine history. No Hausa lore link was verified. |
| **6. Fennec fox** | Compact body, two tall triangular ears and long black-tipped tail; warm sand coat with a green shoulder panel and tiny gold fastening. Keep eyes narrow enough for an adult editorial tone. | An observant listener and traveler; quiet curiosity rather than a mischievous stock fox. | Ears orient to a new location; follows a caravan route on the map; brushes sand away to reveal a diagram. This is metaphorical staging, not archaeology presented as fact. | Immediately legible ears and a good visual connection to North African/Saharan episodes. Risk: a desert-only visual identity could narrow a continent-wide brand; rounded proportions may read like a game companion. No Hausa mythology was verified. |

## Adult audiences across Africa, Asia and the West

These are design hypotheses, not evidence from audience testing. No broad region has a single response to an animal. Each option should be tested with adults who watch history in several countries and languages; African respondents should include Hausa viewers and viewers from other traditions.

| Candidate | Likely communication advantage across markets — hypothesis | What to test with African adult viewers | What to test with Asian and Western adult viewers |
|---|---|---|
| Hare | Ears and body direction communicate curiosity without dialogue. | Does the Hausa connection feel meaningful and accurately framed? Does it suggest a familiar figure when that is intended? | Does it read as an adult guide in stillness, and does the design avoid recalling an existing rabbit mascot? |
| Spider | Lines and nodes explain connections visually. | Are Gizo references recognizable to relevant viewers without suggesting an invented pan-African canon? | Does the stylization reduce aversion while still reading as a spider? Can viewers track the eight limbs on a phone? |
| Tortoise | Stable shape and deliberate gestures suit reflective pacing. | Does a Yoruba attribution feel precise? Is the character more individual than a generic “wise elder”? | Does it feel composed and alert rather than slow, geriatric or preschool-oriented? |
| Hornbill | Large bill and upright stance remain recognizable at small sizes. | Does a zoological character work without borrowing Senufo sacred authority or motifs? | Is it distinguishable from existing bird mascots, and is the bill expressive without constant slapstick? |
| African grey | A speaking bird makes the narrator’s role easy to understand. | Does the character evoke a living African environment rather than an exotic pet stereotype? | Do source-comparison scenes communicate critical judgment rather than mimicry or pirate comedy? |
| Fennec | Ears provide a clear shape and useful gestures. | Does the desert association fit the channel’s full geographic ambitions? | Does it read as a history guide rather than fantasy, gaming or plush merchandise? |

Show the same 8–12 second map explanation with each candidate, plus a still portrait and a serious passage. Ask unaided what age group and subject viewers expect. Measure source recall, clarity, warmth and distinctiveness before asking which animal they like.

## Shared behavior and identity rules

- **Historical gravity:** During enslavement, war, famine, massacre, religious persecution or bereavement, the guide’s movement settles. It can move to the edge of a map, look toward the evidence, or leave the frame. No pratfalls, costume jokes, death reactions played for laughs, or mascot reenactment of victims. Human testimony, documentary material and clear narration lead those passages.
- **Narrative reliability:** The guide distinguishes “a recorded story says,” “historians argue,” and “we do not know.” Folklore appears with a place, language/community, teller or source, and date where known. Historical scenes and folklore scenes need visibly different framing.
- **Green/gold/Georgia:** Use the existing Taswira green and gold as the design’s anchor; final hex values should come from the actual brand assets. Georgia belongs in editorial titles, map captions and source cards, not tiny lettering on the animal. Give skin/fur/feathers enough neutral contrast to work against a green background.
- **Original design process:** Start from animal anatomy and the job it performs. Make black silhouettes first, then a front/side/three-quarter turnaround, five expressions, six functional gestures and a neutral serious pose. Check thumbnails against other existing characters. Do not trace channel assets, museum forms, source illustrations or modern folktale-book artwork.
- **Cultural detail:** Avoid dressing every animal in a grab bag of African patterns or making an accessory from religious regalia. A plain contemporary field satchel or geometric brand pin is sufficient. If a specific textile, hat or object is proposed later, research its place, use and meaning first.
