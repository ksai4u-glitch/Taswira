# Hausa states and Sokoto: research viability memo

Research checked 3 November 2026. Read-only research; no media generated, purchased, uploaded, or externally messaged. Local chapter JSON was used as a lead and comparison target, never independent historical proof. The latest repository state is outside this memo's remit.

## Recommendation

Sokoto is a strong follow-up to Kano. Commission a bounded founding story, provisionally **Sokoto: The Burden of Reform, 1804–1817**, rather than compressing the entire nineteenth century into one rise-and-fall episode. Its central research question is how a scholarly religious community became a conquering government, and who gained protection, authority, or an education while others were captured and enslaved. The question connects intellectual history, war, law, family, and political succession without requiring invented private conversations.

Retain Kano as the audience's point of entry: a city already encountered in the pilot becomes one centre within a larger political order. Conclude the founding episode with the problem of succession and rule; reserve Nana Asma'u's mature teaching network and the developed slave economy for a second Sokoto episode. The checked sources below make these credible research commissions, but several full texts still need acquisition before a final scene-by-scene script.

## Eight principal sources and their actual access status

**R1. Last, Murray. _The Sokoto Caliphate_. London: Longmans, Green and Co., 1967. Ibadan History Series.** [Author-authorized International African Institute edition](https://www.internationalafricaninstitute.org/publishing/sokoto_caliphate); [actual PDF](https://www.internationalafricaninstitute.org/downloads/sokoko_caliph.pdf). **Original scan excerpts visually checked.** The 20,981,408-byte, 372-PDF-page file downloaded successfully in the read-only research sandbox. It has no usable text layer. Visually checked title/copyright, contents, and printed pages 23, 24, 39, 41, 42, 47, and 60; these page numbers are observed, not guessed. A foundational manuscript-and-oral-history study; use alongside newer work and evidence beyond governing households. The official landing page licenses the edition CC BY-NC-ND 3.0; research access does not itself settle reuse of illustrations.

**R2. Sutton, J. E. G. “Towards a Less Orthodox History of Hausaland.” _The Journal of African History_ 20, no. 2 (1979): 179–201.** [Publisher extract and reference list](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-african-history/article/abs/towards-a-less-orthodox-history-of-hausaland/7DAC716970D5D7940ACA83020F8D0070); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0021853700017011). **Substantial publisher extract checked; full article not accessed.** It questions inherited origin narratives and treats the bakwai traditions as political-cultural charters rather than literal founding reportage. Its particular historical model is an argument within historiography, not an uncontested replacement chronology. Useful for avoiding a single timeless “Hausa kingdom,” and for taking archaeology, language, countryside, and state formation seriously.

**R3. Asma'u, Nana. _Collected Works of Nana Asma'u, Daughter of Usman 'dan Fodiyo (1793–1864)_. Edited by Jean Boyd and Beverly B. Mack. East Lansing: Michigan State University Press, 1997.** [Publisher description and contents](https://msupress.org/9781609170653/collected-works-of-nana-asmau/). **Metadata, description, and contents checked; collection itself not read.** The publisher distinguishes the 1997 hardback from its 2012 digital edition. Contents include histories, elegies, instruction, exchanges, manuscript facsimiles, and a chronology: a deep primary-source base for a later education-and-state episode. [Boston University's excerpt record](https://open.bu.edu/items/2397dd01-c7a2-447c-b675-e793e5b9751a) appeared in search, but opening returned HTTP 403; do not claim its Ajami samples were inspected. Nor should a contents title be treated as proof of a work's date or factual claims.

**R4. Mack, Beverly B., and Jean Boyd. _One Woman's Jihad: Nana Asma'u, Scholar and Scribe_. Bloomington: Indiana University Press, 2000.** [George Mason University/Roy Rosenzweig Center teaching excerpt](https://chnm.gmu.edu/wwh/p/214.html). **Long scholarly excerpts and embedded translated verse checked; full book not read.** The site identifies the selected book passages. The analysis describes trained itinerant women teachers, recitation, household instruction, and the education of captured Hausa people. It dates the Hausa poem _The Path of Truth_ to 1842. That creates a securely later setting for an instructional scene; it cannot furnish speech for an 1804 scene. Treat the book's sympathetic interpretation as scholarship to test, and distinguish its illustrative reconstructions from translated verses.

**R5. Mack, Beverly. “Nana Asma'u bint Usman ‘dan Fodio.” _Oxford Bibliographies in Islamic Studies_. Published 15 January 2019.** [Publisher introduction](https://academic.oup.com/reference/62361/reference-article-abstract/554577767); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/obo/9780195390155-0262). **Introduction/extract checked; full bibliography restricted.** Especially useful for chronology and linguistic precision: 1793–1864/65, with the death-year variation explicitly attributed to calendrical concordance; literacy in Fulfulde, Arabic, and Hausa written in Arabic script; working spoken Tamchek. It also situates her education within a family and Qadiriyya tradition. Do not convert linguistic competence into four equally documented written corpora.

**R6. Lovejoy, Paul E., and Jan S. Hogendorn. _Slow Death for Slavery: The Course of Abolition in Northern Nigeria, 1897–1936_. Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 1993, chapter 1, “Slavery and the British Conquest of Northern Nigeria.”** [Publisher chapter summary and bibliographic record](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/slow-death-for-slavery/slavery-and-the-british-conquest-of-northern-nigeria/29C83D13B37FFE405ECB0E18A571A7E7); [chapter DOI](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511563065.002). **Publisher summary and contents checked; full chapter/book not read.** The summary explicitly identifies war, raiding, and tribute as processes of enslavement, and gives a very broad late-century estimate: over one million, perhaps over 2.5 million. Attribute this estimate, retain its range and late date, and never project it onto 1804. The book's date span and contents establish an important research direction: British conquest did not produce one instantaneous transition to freedom.

**R7. Stilwell, Sean, Ibrahim Hamza, and Paul E. Lovejoy. “The Oral History of Royal Slavery in the Sokoto Caliphate: An Interview with Sallama Dako.” _History in Africa_ 28 (2001): 273–291.** [Publisher extract and references](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/history-in-africa/article/abs/oral-history-of-royal-slavery-in-the-sokoto-caliphate-an-interview-with-sallama-dako/16A605A79BC16DD2E6ADBA2E4BDF2F82); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.2307/3172218). **Scholarly introductory extract checked; interview transcript itself not accessed.** It describes powerful enslaved palace officials and the evidentiary silences around them, and explains how descendant/titleholder oral histories can help recover work, farms, and political culture in Kano. This is the strongest explicit Kano-to-Sokoto source bridge found here. Do not invent testimony in Dako's voice or treat privileged palace slavery as typical of all enslaved people. Cambridge's 2014 online publication date is not the article's original year.

**R8. Lofkrantz, Jennifer. “Protecting Freeborn Muslims: The Sokoto Caliphate's Attempts to Prevent Illegal Enslavement and its Acceptance of the Strategy of Ransoming.” _Slavery & Abolition_ 32, no. 1 (2011): 109–127.** [Publisher record/abstract](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/0144039X.2011.538201); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/0144039X.2011.538201). **Indexed publisher abstract checked; direct publisher opening returned HTTP 403; full text unavailable.** [US EPA HERO bibliographic record](https://hero.epa.gov/reference/7563056/) independently confirms author, year, page span, and DOI. The abstract argues that protection of freeborn Muslims was a significant policy concern, while internal constraints prevented the government from stopping their enslavement or consistently securing free release. This supports a carefully attributed reform-versus-practice tension; specific legal cases require the full article and underlying documents.

## Chronology and local-snapshot cautions

The following corrections derive from visually checked R1 scan pages, and should enter the repository audit as source comparisons rather than silent replacements:

| Event or assertion | Defensible treatment |
|---|---|
| Hijra from Degel to Gudu | February 1804. Last p.23 records 21 versus 23 February; p.24 explains a staged migration. Do not assign the same exact day unquestioningly to Yunfa's declaration of war. |
| Alkalawa and Yunfa | Last p.39 gives 3 October 1808, with 26 September as an alternative in its note. The town was not wholly destroyed. |
| Zaria | Local JSON says 1805. Last p.39 instead places its fall about three months after Alkalawa; flag as a chronology requiring reconciliation. |
| Sokoto | Bello's headquarters from autumn 1809, following a camp the previous year: R1 p.41. |
| Abdullahi and Gwandu | The 1812 division is distinct from residence: R1 pp.41–42 places him at Bodinga, with Gwandu his headquarters only after Usman's death. Local “ruled from Gwandu by 1812” conflates them. |
| Usman | R1 p.60 gives 20 April 1817, while discussing calendrical variants. Use 1817 in uncomplicated screen labels. |

The scan also cautions against making a leadership title more precise than the eyewitness accounts: R1 p.24 says the precise salutation is not supplied by its two eyewitness sources. Broader claims that *every* emir was appointed in the same way, travelled annually, or delivered identical tribute need separate emirate-specific checking. No exact Kwatto battlefield position was verified.

Nana Asma'u was approximately ten or eleven in 1804, calculated from the checked birth year in R5. Her mature teaching institution must not be backdated into the opening war. The often repeated “around 1830” foundation date appears on the [African Studies Centre Leiden library page](https://ascleiden.nl/content/library-weekly/nana-asmau), but that biographical paragraph explicitly cites Wikipedia. Treat it as a catalogue lead, not independent corroboration. For the script, use the later educational career verified in R4/R5; acquire R3's chronology before locking a founding year. The translated 1842 instructional work is firmer than a precisely dated inaugural classroom.

## Slavery, religion, and the historical argument

The honest distinction is between protecting people recognized as freeborn Muslims and ending slavery for everyone. R8 documents the former as a contested political project; R6 establishes continued enslavement within the resulting state. Investigate how rulers, scholars, captives, and neighbouring communities disputed status and legitimate war. Religious reform cannot be reduced to an ethnic appetite for conquest, and sincerely held reform cannot excuse the violence it enabled.

R4 presents women's scholarship as community work and describes the incorporation of captives through religious instruction. That conjunction is an excellent adult documentary question: access to education and the coercive conditions of incorporation must be visible together. Neither source supports calling Nana Asma'u an abolitionist. R7 likewise allows the audience to understand enslaved people's political agency without converting ownership into freedom. Any treatment of concubinage and sexual coercion should acquire dedicated scholarship and relevant legal/social evidence before detailed scenes; the checked extracts here cannot supply an individual woman's experience.

## Other Hausa stories: strengths and limits

These are commissioning judgments and research directions, not pre-cleared episode claims.

| Subject | Story potential and map relationship | Research readiness |
|---|---|---|
| Gobir | Best bridge to Sokoto: Degel, political protection/persecution, migration, Alkalawa, displaced power. Distinguish the polity from successive capitals. | Strongest after Kano through R1; requires local perspectives outside the victors' accounts. |
| Katsina | Scholarship, markets, dynastic memory, and relations with Kano; useful alternative to making every Hausa city a preliminary chapter of Sokoto. | Promising; R2 supplies method, not a sufficient city history. Commission a separate chronicle/manuscript/archaeology dossier. |
| Zazzau/Zaria | Gendered dynastic memory and the danger of treating Amina traditions as a securely dated biography; later jihad offers a separate story. | High dramatic promise, higher source-risk. Do not merge incompatible Amina chronologies or select an exact reign for convenience. |
| Daura | The making and political use of origin stories; compare a remembered well and royal genealogies with historical evidence. | Strong interpretive short episode. Present Bayajidda as tradition with variants, not filmed eyewitness history. |
| Rano and Biram | Smaller centres can restore a plural landscape and challenge a story dominated by surviving great capitals. | Better within a comparative Hausa episode until a dedicated source base is obtained. The seven occupational “tasks” need to remain attributed tradition. |

R2's value is precisely that linguistic-cultural belonging and a multi-state political landscape need explaining. The sevenfold schema should not become a literal census, a common constitution, or seven fixed ethnic territories. Include rural populations, migration, changing alignments, and different religious practices. Local “the cities stayed separate until 1804” needs qualification: that date begins a process, not a uniform simultaneous political merger.

## First Sokoto episode: research spine

1. **A community leaves.** Open with the source problem as well as the movement: material circumstances can be reconstructed, but exact departures and motives differ by account. Named people and a few verified places provide anticipation without invented dialogue.
2. **What did reform promise?** Examine preaching, scholarship, authority, and recognized Muslim status. Obtain the reformers' own texts in translation before assigning their arguments to narration.
3. **War changes the community.** Use a small number of decisive, securely sourced transitions; show captives and displacement alongside military outcomes. Avoid a map that paints every Hausa polity one colour in one beat.
4. **A town and a government.** Distinguish where the Shehu, Bello, and Abdullahi lived from how power was divided. Make administration a historical problem, not an inevitable empire montage.
5. **The founder dies; the promise is inherited.** End in 1817 with succession and the work of governing still unresolved. A brief, openly dated forward glance can promise the later questions of education, slavery, and the frontier.

For a later second episode, **Sokoto: Knowledge and Captivity, c.1830–1865** is more coherent than forcing Nana Asma'u's mature work into the founding war. R3–R8 make that scope viable. The British conquest and the prolonged transformation of slavery warrant their own c.1897–1936 episode, with colonial coercion examined rather than an untested liberation ending.
