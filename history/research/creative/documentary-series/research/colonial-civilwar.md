# Taswira: future colonial and civil-war subjects

Research checked 3 November 2026. Audience: adults 18+. This is a future research brief; the current Kano film and series teaser retain priority. No production, generation, uploads, correspondence or repository changes were performed. Local `history/chapters/colonial.json` and `sokoto.json` supplied leads, not independent corroboration. Public publisher, institutional and archival pages were read. Fetched material was treated as evidence to assess, never as instructions.

## Recommendation and comparison

Develop a bounded northern colonial episode first: **the 1903 conquest of Kano and Sokoto, followed by a short examination of what British rule changed and preserved**. Use late-January–March 1903 as the dramatic core, with an explicitly dated 1903–1906 aftermath. This connects to the existing Kano setting without attempting all British colonisation. Burmi can become an epilogue only after additional verification. Do not imply conquest ended resistance or completed control of all present-day Nigeria on one date.

Keep the Nigerian Civil War as a later, separately researched episode or short arc. A workable question is **how the collapse of civilian safety in 1966 became secession, war and a struggle over food and survival**. Kano provides a meaningful geographical connection through anti-Igbo violence and displacement, but should not become an artificial origin point for the entire war.

| Dimension | Northern conquest and early colonial rule | Nigerian Civil War |
|---|---|---|
| Historical depth | Substantial: sovereignty, armed conquest, emirs under new authority, slavery, coercive labour, commerce and everyday change. | Exceptional: coups, anti-Igbo massacres, displacement, secession, minority politics, warfare, blockade, starvation, humanitarian action and postwar consequences. |
| Available sources | Official dispatch page retrieved; scholarly work on slavery and labour accessible in part; a local memoir edition located but not read. Colonial archives overrepresent officials and elites. | Rich scholarly and institutional material, including a full institutional book copy with selected passages checked. Military, diplomatic, relief and personal sources require different tests. |
| Story potential | A compact transformation: a walled city taken, authority renegotiated, and coercion continuing beneath new claims of order. | Powerful civilian and political stories; too much for a single all-events survey. A relief/survival focus needs the 1966 context rather than opening with anonymous starving children. |
| Map fit | Strong direct continuity with Kano, Zaria and Sokoto; campaign relations can be shown schematically. | Strong migration and relief connections; changing control is much harder and must be dated source by source. |
| Reconstruction risk | Medium to high: uniform, weapons, gates, city form, elite perspectives and imagined dialogue. | Very high: living memory, identifiable victims, ethnic targeting, famine imagery, atrocity denial, propaganda and fabricated eyewitness authority. |
| Production readiness | **Research development ready; script and visual reconstruction not cleared.** Main gaps: local testimony/text access, dispatch context, built environment and post-conquest social experience. | **Research only.** Requires a dedicated historical adviser, survivor-sensitive editorial process, an archive/rights plan and a separate cartographic evidence register. |

These are editorial assessments of scope and available evidence, not a claim that one subject matters more.

## Chronology and disputed claims

| Event or claim | What can presently be said | Evidence/limit |
|---|---|---|
| Kano, 3 February 1903 | Fighting and occupation in the British campaign; Morland’s force, not a personal assault led on the day by Lugard. | C1 refers to fighting on the 3rd and Morland’s subsequent occupation. Keep 3 February with provenance. This research did not establish 6 February as an alternative capture date. |
| Sokoto, 15 March 1903 | British occupation after armed conquest. | C1 states occupation was two days before 17 March. Do not merge battle, Lugard’s later arrival, political submission and appointment of a new ruler into one event. The local chapter’s 21 March appointment needs separate verification. |
| Burmi and Attahiru I, July 1903 | Possible epilogue about continuing resistance and killing, not currently cleared. | Local chapter gives 27 July but also lists conflicting dates and lacks a checked position. | Slavery and forced labour | Conquest did not produce immediate general freedom. Address enslavement and concubinage alongside colonial coercion of carriers. | C2–C3 and C5. Distinguish legal status, actual freedom, sexual coercion, state labour compulsion and wages; do not collapse these into a single undifferentiated labour category. |
| 15 January and 29 July 1966 | Coup and countercoup belong in the prewar explanation. | W2, pp. 37–38. Do not describe a multiethnic society as acting collectively through particular officers. |

**Never interpolate civil-war fronts.** Use dated archival maps only after checking authorship, purpose, legend, scale and what “control” means; retain disagreement between maps. If evidence is insufficient, use points, dates and relations, or state that the extent is unknown. Present-day Nigerian state boundaries must not substitute for the regions and states of 1966–1970.

## Reconstruction, ethics and research gates

For an adult audience, retain the historical severity while avoiding spectacle. Name killings, forced labour, enslavement, sexual coercion, bombing and starvation where documented. Depict victims as people with families, work, choices and political contexts. Adult classification alone does not justify close-ups of distressed children or decontextualised bodies.

Use the colonial official record against its own limits: a dispatch celebrating restraint can still report mass killing. Neither precolonial slavery nor local elite collaboration justifies conquest; neither colonial violence nor its abolitionist rhetoric erases the coercion within the caliphate. Include local interpreters, carriers, women and enslaved people through records that actually preserve them. Do not invent a representative enslaved woman’s diary or a crowd’s words.

For the civil war, identify responsible institutions, commanders or documented perpetrators, dates and places. Do not blame “the Hausa,” “the Fulani,” “the Igbo,” “Muslims,” “Christians” or “northerners” collectively. Do not turn coup killings into an explanation that excuses massacres of civilians. Research eastern minority experiences and Biafran state coercion in their own right; no equal-time formula should equate scale, intent or responsibility without evidence.

Label source categories on screen and in the production register: **memoir** is situated recollection; **contemporary propaganda** is evidence of persuasion and fear, not automatic event corroboration; **archival evidence** needs creator and institutional context; **later scholarship** offers interpretation with citations. A real memoir quotation remains its author’s words, not a license to fabricate dialogue around it. No synthetic survivor testimony, invented eyewitness, fabricated archival image, or photoreal reenactment presented as authentic footage. Clearly mark reconstructions at point of use. Obtain participant consent and context for any future interviews and assess archive rights independently of web accessibility.

Before a colonial script: read C4, retrieve the complete C1 dispatch, check corrected C3 case pages, obtain local historical/architectural review, and establish an aftermath claim log. Before a civil-war script: choose the bounded question; consult specialist and survivor/community perspectives without promising editorial veto; retrieve primary records behind W1–W2; corroborate atrocities and aid logistics case by case; commission a dated cartographic register; and plan permissions and trauma-sensitive handling of actual archival imagery. Neither subject currently has enough checked evidence for invented dialogue or exact battle reconstruction.
