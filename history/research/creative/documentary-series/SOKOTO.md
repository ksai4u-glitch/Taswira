# Sokoto: The Burden of Reform, 1804–1817

3 November 2026. **Research development begun; original opening draft, not a locked screenplay or a recorded narration.** Current Kano work and the separate Taswira teaser retain production priority.

## Question and scope

How did a scholarly religious community become a conquering government, and how did its promises of legitimate rule coexist with captivity and enslavement? Begin with the departure from Degel in February 1804; end with Usman's death and the succession problem in 1817. Explain that political formation occurred over time and differed by place. A brief, explicitly later epilogue can introduce the unresolved work of governing.

Do not reduce religious reform to ethnic motive, or treat sincere religious argument as an excuse for violence. Investigate the reformers' own writings, rival claims to religious legitimacy, legal status and the experience of people brought under the new authorities. The available founding accounts disproportionately preserve governing households; acknowledge that imbalance.

## Original opening narration draft

Roughly 90 seconds, pending a scratch read. Source notes below are not spoken. The language is original, not purported contemporary testimony.

> February, 1804. In Gobir, a community begins to leave.
>
> From Degel, the followers of Usman dan Fodio make for Gudu. The movement takes place in stages. Even the accounts of its date do not quite agree.
>
> Last's reconstruction describes a threat of attack, a search for safety, negotiations breaking down. The departure becomes a turning point in a struggle over faith and authority.
>
> Within a few years, war will carry that struggle through the Hausa states. At Alkalawa, a ruler will die, people will be taken captive, and refugees will move towards other Gobir towns.
>
> A new order is taking shape. Its history will be written in arguments about government, in the growth of towns, and in lives changed by conquest.
>
> Who could claim protection? Who could be enslaved? And what happened when promises of reform became the work of ruling?
>
> To understand the world that formed around Sokoto, we begin with the people leaving Degel, and with the accounts that allow us to follow them.

Paragraphs 1–3: R1 pp.23–24. Paragraph 4: R1 p.39, with broad chronology from the checked founding narrative. Paragraph 5: editorial framing developed from R1 pp.41–42. Paragraph 6: research questions informed by R6/R8, not a claim that their later evidence describes a particular 1804 captive. Paragraph 7: original transition. No detailed eyewitness claim, casualty total, exact route or private speech is introduced.

“Followers” follows the source's self-identification. It does not imply that all Muslims joined the movement or that its opponents lacked Muslim identities. Scripts must distinguish historical religious claims from the narrator's labels.

## Five-part structure

1. **Leaving Degel.** Community, political setting and a staged departure. Use named places and visibly approximate connections. Explain the source problem alongside the opening movement.
2. **The arguments of reform.** Place religious teaching and political legitimacy in context using the reformers' actual writings and critical scholarship. Primary-text acquisition is a real dependency, not a space to fill from general memory.
3. **War and its consequences.** Follow selected, corroborated changes rather than a map colouring every polity at once. Include loss, captivity and displacement; avoid a royal victory montage as the episode's whole point of view.
4. **Making a government.** Bello, Usman and Abdullahi; settlements, residence, authority and supervision. Make different centres visible without inventing boundaries or administrative uniformity.
5. **1817 and inheritance.** Succession and the unresolved tension between legitimacy, protection and coercion. A clearly dated forward glance can set up education, slavery and later frontier histories.
