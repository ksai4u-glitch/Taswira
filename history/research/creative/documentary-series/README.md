## Editorial standard

Use three independent descriptors for each consequential claim: **source type**, **degree of confidence**, and **reconstruction status**. A written account can be partisan; archaeological evidence can have a disputed identification; a firm event can still have an uncertain location. Keep those distinctions visible in the claim record and, where they affect understanding, in the narration or on-screen label.

Name violence, enslavement, sexual coercion, conquest, religious conflict, colonial force, massacres and starvation when the evidence warrants it. Explain actors, causes, institutions and consequences. Attribute responsibility to documented people and institutions; avoid collective ethnic or religious blame. An adult audience permits difficult history and careful language. It does not make invented atrocities, victim spectacle or fabricated eyewitness testimony acceptable.

Present myths and traditions as traditions with variants. Present a source's numbers as its claims unless independently supported. Preserve disagreement and distinguish an estimate from an audited count. Never manufacture archival footage, first-person testimony, private dialogue or a survivor's memories. Label generated reconstructions at point of use; list historical and creative sources separately.

## A distinctive narration voice

Arthur remains the narrator. Calm, concrete explanation should carry most of an episode; a small number of poetic lines give transitions and endings their force. Introduce a person, place or material detail before an abstract question. Let suspense come from historical choices and consequences, with room for silence. Answer a question before stacking another riddle on top of it.

Write sources into the syntax where needed: “The Chronicle credits…”, “Last places…”, “The date is disputed…”. Avoid treating a later court account as a camera recording. Use changing pace and emphasis rather than continuous declamation. Obtain a pronunciation reference for Kano, Gijimasu, Rumfa, Kurmi, Usman dan Fodio, Abdullahi, Alkalawa and other proper names before a new voice master is locked; automatic transcription is not a pronunciation review.

## Visual and sound language

Retain the approved warm earth palette, broad kingdom scale, varied gates, distant human activity and coherent forward travel. Streets can bring viewers nearer to landmarks while faces remain background. Name a landmark only when the image, chronology and source support the identification. Generated skyline features are not measured terrain.

Animate gate or landmark formation deterministically in the native scene when possible. Stable geometry, an established camera path and locked end frames should carry a demanding transition. Use image/video generation selectively for atmosphere and motion that can be inspected, without making the city change shape. A clean motivated edit is preferable to retaining a visible failure for the sake of a purported continuous flight.

The latest revision requires score continuously from frame one under narration, superseding the earlier deliberate music gap. Treat the current licensed orchestral score and modern kakaki accent as a creative soundtrack, not recovered medieval music. The longer series can develop a distinctive musical identity through cleared recordings and collaboration; no contact or commission has been made in this stage.

## Research and production loop

Research question → claim-and-source record → map relationship check → original script → timed board/native camera proof → approved references → live cost quote and capped generation → picture review → voice/pronunciation review → mix/captions/attribution → full playback → owner review → publication decision.

Each episode must leave reusable research and a production record: source passages and access limits; entities and date ranges; disputed interpretations; approved and rejected shot intervals; camera settings; voice script versions; rights and attribution; actual credit spend; and lessons that change the next episode's process. Templates improve consistency, but each kingdom needs its own geography, architecture and political history.
